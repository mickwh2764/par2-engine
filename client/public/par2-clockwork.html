How Your Body Keeps Time

Your body runs on a 24-hour clock — but it's not one clock, it's dozens. Every organ has its own timekeeper, and they're all synchronised by the brain. This diagram shows that hierarchy, and what happens when it breaks down.

Organ clocks
Each coloured ring is an organ with its own clock. The further out, the harder it is to shift — lung (outermost) takes 3.3× longer to adjust than the brain. Hover any ring.
4
The brain master clock
The blue centre (SCN) is the brain's master clock. Paradoxically it resets the fastest — it's a sensitive sensor, not a rigid keeper. The gold dashed ring marks where most clock genes cluster.

What is PAR(2)?
PAR(2) is a mathematical framework that fits each organ's 24-hour gene expression data to a simple model (an AR(2) process), then measures how "sticky" each organ's clock is. The stickiness is the eigenvalue |λ| — a number between 0 and 1. Higher = harder to shift = more inertial. The rings in this diagram are placed at each organ's measured |λ|. The needle on the gauge above tracks whichever ring you're hovering.
Disease mode simulates what happens in cancer or metabolic disease — the hierarchy collapses as peripheral organ clocks drift toward |λ| = 1 (runaway) while the SCN loses its ability to dominate.
